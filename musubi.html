Artist's Statement

Musubi is an interactive digital work that invites the viewer to surface fragments of feeling from Makoto Shinkai's Your Name (2016). Two worlds — Taki's urban Tokyo and Mitsuha's rural Itomori — sit divided on screen, separated by a thin line of light, but connected by the comet and the sky. Each image can be explored to reveal its full width, as if searching for something just out of frame. Each click surfaces a memory fragment: a name fading from a palm, the taste of something unplaceable, a call that doesn't connect. A red thread slowly draws between them.

And yet the work ends before the embrace.

The final image is the moment on the mountain — Taki and Mitsuha facing each other at last, after years of searching across time and forgetting. They have found each other. They are close enough to touch. I chose the frame just before they do. That gap between outstretched hands is not an accident of timing. It is the argument. The reaching is the work. The closing of the distance would end the longing, and longing is what Musubi is about.

This work is grounded in one central critical question: why has anime — a medium of extraordinary visual and emotional craft — been so persistently excluded from serious art historical discourse? Shiner's account of the "great division" offers a starting point. The separation of "fine art" from "craft" was never a natural distinction; it was a constructed hierarchy that decided whose ways of making counted, and whose did not. Anime has long sat on the wrong side of that line, dismissed as popular entertainment rather than legitimate artistic practice. But this is a judgment worth challenging.

Japanese artist Takashi Murakami made exactly that challenge. His Superflat theory, introduced in 2000, argued that anime and manga were not shallow departures from serious art, but the continuation of a deep visual lineage — one traceable through Edo-period ukiyo-e woodblock prints, with their strong outlines, flat areas of colour, and surface-oriented compositions that guide the eye across the image rather than into it. Murakami identified in this flatness not a deficiency but a distinct Japanese aesthetic tradition that predated Western perspective entirely, and that survived — transformed — into postwar commercial culture. To dismiss anime, then, is to dismiss an entire visual history.

Your Name sits squarely within this lineage. Shinkai's signature style — luminous skies, flattened depth, light dissolving on water — reads less like commercial animation and more like a moving ukiyo-e: an image of a floating world, beautiful and impermanent. The film's visuals are not decorative. They carry feeling as their primary content. And yet, despite its extraordinary reach — seen by millions across Japan, the Philippines, Brazil, and beyond — Your Name has rarely been taken seriously as an art object. The V&A's 2023 exhibition Japan: Myths to Manga traced this lineage from folklore through ukiyo-e to anime, institutionally affirming what Murakami had argued for decades: that the canon had been drawing the wrong boundary all along.

Musubi makes that argument interactively. The viewer is not asked to contemplate the film from a distance. They are asked to reach into it — to click, to surface a memory, to draw a thread between two worlds that can never quite close. The format enacts what the film already knows: that these images are not made for disinterested aesthetic appreciation. They pull the body into feeling. And if Shiner is right — that the division between art and craft was always about who gets to feel, and whose feeling counts — then the question this work asks is simple: why did it take so long for anime to be let in?

The choice to end on a gap rather than an embrace is deliberate. Musubi — meaning both "binding" and "the flow of time" — is never fully drawn. Some distances, this piece suggests, cannot be closed. But the reaching matters. That is what the red thread is for.
