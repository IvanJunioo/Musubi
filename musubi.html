Musubi

an interactive work on memory, longing, and the viral image
after Makoto Shinkai's Your Name (君の名は, 2016)

Shiner

Art vs. Craft

Anime has long been excluded from the "fine art" canon — a hierarchy Shiner reveals as constructed, not natural. The same divide that separated "fine" from "useful" now separates prestige cinema from animation. This work insists on anime's legitimacy as a site of serious aesthetic and critical inquiry.

Porras

Virality

Like the Hispano-Filipino ivory crucifix traveling the Manila Galleon route — reinterpreted at every point of contact — Your Name spread through informal networks: fan communities, social media, word of mouth. Shinkai's images were never static. They migrated across cultures, arriving transformed.

Images

The Significance of Images

Shinkai's images carry longing as their primary content. The twilight glow of Itomori, the comet, the name on the palm — these are not merely decorative. They are vehicles of affect and cultural memory that cross borders without translation, reaching viewers who have never seen a Japanese film before.

Artist's Statement

Musubi is an interactive digital work that invites the viewer to surface fragments of feeling from Makoto Shinkai's Your Name (2016). Two worlds — Taki's urban Tokyo and Mitsuha's rural Itomori — sit divided on screen, separated by a thin line of light, but is connected by the comet and the sky. Each image can be explored to reveal its full width, as if searching for something just out of frame. Each click surfaces a memory fragment: a name fading from a palm, the taste of something unplaceable, a call that doesn't connect. A red thread slowly draws between them.

And yet the work ends before the embrace.

The final image is the moment on the mountain — Taki and Mitsuha facing each other at last, after years of searching across time and forgetting. They have found each other. They are close enough to touch. I chose the frame just before they do. That gap between outstretched hands is not an accident of timing. It is the argument. The reaching is the work. The closing of the distance would end the longing, and longing is what Musubi is about.

The work draws on three critical frameworks from this course. First, Shiner's account of the "great division" between art and craft — the constructed hierarchy that decided whose ways of making counted as "art." Anime, like the quilts Faith Ringgold used to challenge the canon, has long been treated as popular entertainment rather than legitimate artistic practice. This work insists otherwise. Second, Porras's framework of virality: Your Name traveled the way the ivory crucifixes did — not through institutional channels but through informal networks, reinterpreted at every crossing, never arriving identical to how it left. Third, and most centrally, the turn toward the significance of images rather than "art". Shinkai's visuals are not made for disinterested aesthetic contemplation. They pull the body into feeling. The interactive format performs this: the viewer reaches toward something that recedes.

The choice to end on a gap rather than an embrace is deliberate. Musubi — meaning both "binding" and "the flow of time" — is never fully drawn. Some distances, this piece suggests, cannot be closed. But the reaching matters. That is what the red thread is for.
